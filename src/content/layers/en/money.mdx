Anyone who earns from crime, or pays for servers and tools, needs money that does not carry their name. Cash does not travel over the internet and bank accounts come with identity checks, so the usual choice is cryptocurrency. The trouble is that on most chains the payment trail is not hidden at all. It is public, permanent and searchable, and only the names are missing.

## Pseudonymous is not anonymous

A Bitcoin address is a pen name. Nothing on the chain says who holds the key behind it, but every transaction that address takes part in is published to everyone, forever. Meiklejohn and colleagues put it in one line in 2013: ownership is implicitly anonymous, but the flow of money is globally visible <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. The analyst's job is to turn many pen names into a few owners.

Two rules of thumb do most of that work. The first is common-input ownership. A transaction that spends coins from several addresses needs a valid signature for each of them, so in ordinary wallets all the inputs belong to one owner <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. The second is change detection. Bitcoin spends whole outputs, so a payment of 0.3 from an output of 1.0 sends the remainder back to the payer, usually to a brand-new address. Spotting which output is the change adds that address to the payer's group.

The second rule is a habit of wallet software, not a property of the protocol, and the paper shows what happens when it is applied carelessly: a naive version fused Mt. Gox, Instawallet, BitPay and Silk Road into one "super-cluster" of 1.6 million public keys <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. With stricter conditions the authors identified 3,540,831 change addresses, at a false-positive rate they estimated at 0.17% <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. Their snapshot of the chain held 16,086,073 transactions and 12,056,684 public keys, which the two rules collapsed into 3,383,904 clusters <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. To put names on clusters they simply did business with services, 344 transactions in all, and the heuristics spread those few labels across more than 1.8 million addresses <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />.

The figure is the whole layer in small. Everything an analyst uses is <span class="hl">readable to anyone</span>: addresses, amounts, times, which outputs feed which inputs. The one thing missing is the owner, and that is supplied by somebody off the chain who already knows it.

Large balances rarely move in one jump. A common pattern is the peel chain: a big output pays a small amount to someone and sends the rest to a fresh change address, which pays another small amount and sends the rest on again <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. Once the change rule is trusted, an analyst can walk the chain hop by hop and read off every recipient. Following the first 100 hops of three peel chains that started from one large wallet, Meiklejohn's team found that 54 of the 300 peels went to exchanges <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />. Their conclusion was that an agency with subpoena power would be well placed to learn who pays whom <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />.

Commercial tools now do this at scale, and courts have examined them. Ruling on the Bitcoin Fog prosecution in February 2024, Judge Randolph Moss found that the government had shown its blockchain analysis was "the product of reliable principles and methods" and said such analysis "is not junk science" <Cite id="sterlingov-reactor-opinion" />. The software had attributed more than 900,000 addresses to the mixer. The same opinion notes that the case did not rest "exclusively, or even primarily" on it: seized devices, forum posts, IP records and manual tracing carried as much weight <Cite id="sterlingov-reactor-opinion" />. That is the realistic picture. Chain analysis produces leads and corroboration. It rarely stands alone.

## What a mixer breaks, and what it leaves

A mixer tries to cut the line between coins going in and coins coming out. There are three designs, and each has a different place where it can be taken apart.

A custodial mixer takes your coins into a pool and later pays out different coins. The link exists only in the operator's records, which makes the operator the weak point. When ChipMixer was taken down on 15 March 2023, police seized four servers, about 7 TB of data and about 1,909 BTC, then worth about EUR 44 million. Europol said the service, running since mid-2017, may have laundered 152,000 BTC <Cite id="money-europol-chipmixer" />. The seized data is the record the mixer existed to hide. The pattern is older than ChipMixer. When the Dutch fiscal police took Bestmixer.io offline on 22 May 2019, they seized six servers in the Netherlands and Luxembourg, said the service had handled at least $200 million in a year, and announced that they had gathered information on transactions between customers and the mixer, to be analysed with Europol and then shared with other countries <Cite id="money-fiod-bestmixer" />. Keeping no records is not a way out either. In October 2020 FinCEN fined Larry Dean Harmon, operator of the Helix mixer, $60 million. Among its findings: he never collected names or addresses on more than 1.2 million transactions and "actively deleted even the minimal customer information" he did collect <Cite id="money-fincen-helix" />.

The operators themselves are usually named by other people's records. Minh Quoc Nguyen, charged in March 2023 as ChipMixer's operator, had repeatedly told forum readers "please do not use AML/KYC exchanges", and once wrote of Coinbase handing data to the IRS: "That's what KYC exchanges lead to. All your data will be shipped to IRS." The complaint against him is built from the business records of hosting companies, PayPal and Google, covering accounts that ran the service under false names. A PayPal account that paid monthly for one of ChipMixer's servers received a $150 card payment from "MINH NGUYEN", and records from Binance showed an account in that name, verified with a Vietnamese government ID card <Cite id="money-chipmixer-complaint" />. Bitcoin Fog ran from 2011 to 2021 and processed more than 1.2 million BTC, about $400 million at the time. Investigators obtained records of its operator's true-name accounts at several exchanges and traced most of what was deposited there back to Bitcoin Fog's clusters <Cite id="money-doj-fog-verdict" />. Roman Sterlingov was convicted by a jury in March 2024 and sentenced on 8 November 2024 to 12 years and 6 months <Cite id="money-bitcoin-fog-sentence" />. He maintained that he was only a user <Cite id="money-bitcoin-fog-user" />.

CoinJoin removes the custodian. Many users sign one shared transaction with equal-sized outputs, which defeats the common-input rule because the inputs really do belong to different people. Someone still has to organise the rounds, though. Samourai Wallet ran such a coordinator. In November 2025 its founders were sentenced, Keonne Rodriguez to five years and William Lonergan Hill to four, for a service that prosecutors said handled more than $237 million in illegal transactions <Cite id="money-samourai-sentence" />. The coordinator is the part a court can reach.

A smart-contract mixer has no operator holding funds at all. Users deposit into a contract and later withdraw to a new address with a zero-knowledge proof. Tornado Cash is the example. Treasury had sanctioned its first mixer, Blender.io, on 6 May 2022, for processing over $20.5 million from the Axie Infinity theft <Cite id="money-ofac-blender" />. On 8 August 2022 it sanctioned Tornado Cash, saying it had laundered more than $7 billion since 2019, including over $455 million stolen by the North Korean Lazarus Group <Cite id="money-ofac-tornado-2022" />. On 26 November 2024 the Fifth Circuit held that its immutable smart contracts were not "property" that sanctions could block <Cite id="money-van-loon" />, and on 21 March 2025 Treasury removed the sanctions <Cite id="money-ofac-tornado-delisting" />. With no server to seize, the criminal case went to a person. In August 2025 a jury convicted developer Roman Storm on one count, conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money-transmitting business, and could not agree on the laundering and sanctions counts. As reported in August 2026, the retrial on those counts is set for 26 April 2027 while his motion for acquittal is still pending <Cite id="money-storm-retrial" />.

Whatever the design, a mixer only hides the path inside it. The amounts going in and out, the times, and above all <span class="hl">where the coins go next</span> stay on the public ledger. A deposit of an unusual size followed shortly by a withdrawal of nearly the same size is a lead no matter how many other users were in the pool.

## Privacy coins: sound math, leaky use

Privacy coins try to fix the ledger itself. Monero hides the real input of each transaction among decoys, called mixins, and gives every payment a one-time address; the [blockchain basics](/basics/ledgers/) page shows the mechanics. Two studies in 2017 measured how well this worked on the chain as it then was, and both found the weakness in how it was used rather than in the cryptography.

The key fact is that an output can be spent only once. Kumar, Fischer, Tople and Saxena analysed every transaction up to 6 February 2017 and found that 65.9% of all inputs had no decoys at all, which made them "traceable by default". Each of those reveals an output that has been spent, so wherever that output appears as a decoy in another ring it can be crossed out. Repeating the step brought the total to 87.9% of inputs traced <Cite id="money-kumar-2017" />. Möser and colleagues, with data to 15 April 2017, found the same cascade: 64.04% of inputs had no decoys, and about 62% of the inputs that did have one or more could be deduced. They also estimated that simply guessing the newest ring member would pick the real input about 80% of the time across all inputs with decoys <Cite id="money-moser-2018" />.

The era matters. Neither cascade touched the newer RingCT transactions: Kumar's attack "did not find results on RingCTs" <Cite id="money-kumar-2017" />, and Möser's team put that down not to RingCT itself but to the fact that it arrived after a two-decoy minimum was already in force <Cite id="money-moser-2018" />. The rules then tightened. From 16 September 2017 only RingCT transactions with rings of at least five were allowed, then at least seven from April 2018, exactly 11 from October 2018 and 16 from August 2022 <Cite id="money-monero-forks" />. The September 2017 release also changed how decoys are picked, based on an early draft of the Möser paper <Cite id="money-moser-2018" />. The well-known percentages are a measurement of early Monero, not of Monero today.

Zcash offers a shielded pool alongside ordinary transparent addresses, and the problem there was how few people used it. Kappos, Yousaf, Maller and Meiklejohn parsed the chain up to 21 January 2018. 73.5% of transactions were fully transparent, and only 14.96% touched the shielded pool at all. Linking deposits by the founders and by miners to later withdrawals accounted for 65.6% of the value withdrawn from the pool, and their simple heuristics shrank the overall anonymity set by 69.1% <Cite id="money-kappos-2018" />.

Even a coin that holds up inside the chain is visible at its edges. In the Incognito Market case, the market's administrator wallet sent bitcoin to a swap service four times between July 2021 and May 2022. Each time, 21 to 40 minutes later, an exchange account received Monero of about the same value: 2 BTC went in on 31 May 2022, were swapped for about 304.74 XMR, and 300 XMR arrived in the account 35 minutes later. The account had been opened with a Taiwanese driver's licence <Cite id="money-incognito-complaint" />. Nobody had to trace anything inside Monero. The way in and the way out were enough.

The other limit is practical. A coin that is hard to trace is also hard to sell at a regulated venue. Binance, for one, delisted Monero in February 2024 <Cite id="money-binance-xmr-delisting" />. That pushes holders back toward a conversion step, and conversion is where the next section begins.

## The exit: where the ledger meets a name

Coins on a chain do not pay rent. At some point they have to become ordinary money, and the businesses that do that conversion are where the ledger meets <span class="leak">an identity document</span>. A regulated exchange asks for ID when an account is opened. Clustering tells an investigator that one owner sent coins to that exchange; the exchange's records say who the owner is.

The volume through that door is large. Chainalysis estimates that flows from illicit sources to centralized exchanges have averaged more than $14 billion a year since 2020. What has changed is the route: direct transfers from illicit addresses made up over 40% of those deposits in 2021 and 2022 and around 15% by the second quarter of 2025, so more money now passes through intermediaries before it arrives <Cite id="money-chainalysis-seizable" />. The intermediaries are increasingly professional. Chainalysis counted $16.1 billion through Chinese-language laundering networks in 2025, about 20% of the laundering it could see, and it calls its overall total a lower bound because it only covers services the company has attributed <Cite id="money-chainalysis-2026" />.

Services that skip those checks are the obvious alternative, and they become targets in their own right. US prosecutors say the Russian exchange Garantex processed at least $96 billion in cryptocurrency transactions from April 2019. On 6 March 2025 the US Secret Service seized its domains, German and Finnish police took its servers, and more than $26 million was frozen <Cite id="money-doj-garantex" />. A look-alike called Grinex appeared within weeks, and Treasury designated it as Garantex's successor in August 2025 <Cite id="money-grinex" />. Rebrands are cheap, but the old and new wallets are connected on the ledger, which is how analysts tied the two together.

Stablecoins add a different kind of chokepoint. A token like USDT is issued by a company that can freeze balances at specific addresses. Just before the Garantex takedown, Tether froze about $27 million of USDT held in its wallets, and the exchange halted withdrawals <Cite id="money-tether-garantex" />. A chain can be permissionless while the asset on it is not.

## Where it gives way

The documented cases fail at the edges of the chain, not in the middle.

<Fig caption="Four cases drawn the same way. What was on the public ledger (yellow) went through steps meant to break the trail, and every row still ends at a business that knew its customer, whose records named someone (magenta)." size="wide">
  <MoneyFunnel lang="en" />
</Fig>

- [Welcome to Video](/cases/#welcome-to-video). The site took at least 420 BTC through about 7,300 transactions. IRS-CI followed the payments to the operator and the server in South Korea, and 337 users were charged around the world <Cite id="money-doj-wtv" />. The site gave every user a fresh deposit address, more than 1.3 million in all. The forfeiture complaint shows how that was undone: the payments came from accounts at three exchanges, and the customer data on the seized server showed which username had made which payment <Cite id="money-wtv-forfeiture" />.
- [Twitter, 2020](/cases/#twitter-2020). Agents traced about 3.69 BTC from the "Chaewon" wallet to another member of the group. Binance records showed that accounts linked to that wallet were controlled by Mason Sheppard, and Coinbase's know-your-customer data "included Mason Sheppard's driver's license, date of birth, and address" <Cite id="case-tw-chainalysis" />.
- [Bitfinex](/cases/#bitfinex). 119,754 BTC were stolen in 2016. In February 2022 agents seized about 94,000 BTC that were still sitting in the wallet that had received them, after getting into files that held the private keys <Cite id="money-doj-bitfinex" />. The coins had been <span class="hl">visible to everyone</span> for six years. The part that had moved had already run into exchanges. Accounts opened under other people's names were frozen in 2017 when the exchange asked for more identification, and a spreadsheet in Ilya Lichtenstein's cloud storage listed them as "FROZEN". Funds also reached accounts in the couple's own names at six other exchanges, and a $500 Walmart gift card bought with traced coins was ordered from an IP address leased in Lichtenstein's name <Cite id="money-bitfinex-sof" />.
- [Incognito Market](/cases/#incognito). Besides the Monero deposits described above, the administrator wallet helped pay for a domain bought through a Namecheap account registered to "RuiSiang Lin", with a Taiwanese phone number and a Taipei address <Cite id="money-incognito-complaint" />.
- Colonial Pipeline. Of the roughly 75 BTC paid in May 2021, the Justice Department seized 63.7 BTC on 7 June after following transfers on the public ledger to an address for which the FBI had the private key. It did not say how it got the key <Cite id="money-doj-colonial" />.
- Bybit. On 21 February 2025 about $1.5 billion in crypto was stolen. Five days later the FBI attributed it to North Korea's "TraderTraitor" actors, published laundering addresses, and noted that the thieves were "proceeding rapidly", dispersing converted assets "across thousands of addresses on multiple blockchains" <Cite id="money-fbi-bybit" />. Within about ten days all of the stolen ether had been swapped, mostly through a cross-chain protocol. Bybit's chief executive said about 77% of the funds were still traceable and about 3% had been frozen <Cite id="money-bybit-laundering" />. Tracing worked. Recovery needs someone at the other end who can freeze, and the thieves moved faster than that.

The pattern is the same throughout. The cryptography held. What gave way was a key, a custodian, or the step where coins had to turn into something spendable.

## Common misconceptions

- "Bitcoin is anonymous." It is pseudonymous. Every payment is public, and clustering has been documented in the open literature since 2013 <Cite id="meiklejohn-2013" />.
- "A mixer makes coins untraceable." It hides one path. Operators and coordinators get seized or prosecuted, their servers hand over the path they hid, and amounts, timing and the exit stay public <Cite id="money-chipmixer" /> <Cite id="money-fiod-bestmixer" /> <Cite id="money-samourai-sentence" />.
- "Tornado Cash was delisted, so the case is over." Removing sanctions is not an acquittal. Roman Storm stands convicted on one count, with a retrial on the others scheduled <Cite id="money-storm-retrial" />.
- "Studies showed Monero is traceable." They showed that early Monero was, through missing decoys and predictable decoy choice, on data that ends in early 2017; the rules changed after that <Cite id="money-kumar-2017" /> <Cite id="money-moser-2018" />. Zcash's weakness was that most people never used its private pool <Cite id="money-kappos-2018" />.
- "Traceable means recoverable." Bybit was traced in near real time and most of it still got away <Cite id="money-fbi-bybit" />.
